Cars get lived in. That’s what they’re for. But somewhere between the dog’s third muddy walk of the week, the school-run snacks and the time someone left a half-drunk coffee in the cup holder over a heatwave, the inside of the car can go from “cosy” to “please don’t look in the back”.

You’re not the first, and you definitely won’t be the last. Here’s how to deal with the three big ones: pet hair, accidents and smells.

## Pet hair

Dog hair is the most common thing we deal with inside cars, and it’s stubborn for a reason. Short, wiry hairs work their way into the weave of seat fabric and carpet like tiny needles. A normal vacuum just skims over the top.

### What works at home

- **Vacuum slowly.** Go against the direction of the pile, in short strokes, with a narrow nozzle.
- **Rubber is your friend.** A damp rubber glove, a rubber pet-hair brush or even a squeegee dragged across the fabric pulls hairs into clumps you can then hoover up.
- **Do the boot properly.** If the dog travels in the boot, lift the floor panel. Hair gets everywhere.
- **Protect from the start.** A washable seat cover or boot liner saves hours later.

## Accidents

Pets get travel sick. Children get travel sick. Shopping bags leak. And sometimes, as one customer found out on a drive to Manchester with a cat who had strong opinions about the trip, a passenger seat gets used for something it was never designed for.

### What to do straight away

1. **Blot, don’t rub.** Rubbing pushes liquid deeper into the foam. Press kitchen roll or an old towel firmly onto it and lift. Repeat.
2. **Remove what you can.** For anything solid, lift it off carefully with a card or scraper before blotting.
3. **Ventilate.** Windows down if it’s safe to leave them. Moisture trapped in a closed car makes smells worse.
4. **Don’t drown it in perfume.** Heavily scented sprays mix with the original smell rather than removing it.
5. **Get it cleaned soon.** The longer an accident sits in seat foam, the harder it is to get out completely.

Urine and sickness soak through the fabric into the foam underneath. Surface cleaning makes it look fine, but the smell returns on the first warm day. A deep clean works on the seat itself, not just the top layer.

## Smells

Most car smells come from something you can’t see: under the seats, in the carpet, in the boot well, or in the foam of a seat. Common culprits:

- **Wet dog.** Damp fur, damp carpets, and a closed car on a sunny day.
- **Spilt milk.** Genuinely one of the worst. It smells fine for a day, then really doesn’t.
- **Food.** Chips down the side of the seat. A forgotten banana. The archaeology of the school run.
- **Damp.** A leaking seal, wet footwells or condensation. Common near the river in Bridgnorth and Ironbridge.
- **Smoke.** Gets into the headlining, seats and vents.

### Why air freshener doesn’t fix it

An air freshener covers a smell for a while. If the source is still in the car, the smell comes back as soon as the freshener runs out. To get rid of a smell, you have to remove what’s causing it.

## Leather seats

Leather is easier to wipe but has its own problems. Hair sticks in the perforations and stitching, spills sit in the creases, and body oils build up over time and dull the finish. It needs proper cleaning and care, not just a wipe with a baby wipe (which can dry it out).
