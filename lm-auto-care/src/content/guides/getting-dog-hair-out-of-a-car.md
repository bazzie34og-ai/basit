There are two kinds of dog owner. The ones whose car is covered in dog hair, and the ones who've given up noticing.

If you're in the north-east with a dog, the car's probably part of the routine: Balmedie on a Sunday, the Deeside Way after work, a wet run round Hazlehead. And every one of those ends with a damp dog in the boot shedding into the carpet. This note covers what actually works for getting the hair out, what doesn't, and the point at which it's worth handing it to us for £10.

## Why dog hair is so hard to shift

Dog hair isn't like dust or crumbs. Each hair is fine, often slightly barbed along its length, and light enough to be pushed down into fabric rather than lying on top of it. Car carpets and the fabric on seats are a tight weave of loops. The hair works its way in between those loops and grips.

That's why you can hoover the boot twice and it still looks grey. The hoover runs over the top. It picks up what's lying loose and leaves everything that's woven in.

Different dogs make different problems. Short, stiff hair, the kind a lot of terriers and Labradors have, sticks into fabric like tiny needles. Long, soft hair from spaniels and collies tangles and balls up. Double-coated dogs moult in clouds twice a year and leave hair in places you didn't know the dog had been.

## What works at home

You don't need anything expensive. Most of the work is loosening the hair so the hoover can take it.

### A rubber glove

The cheapest tool and one of the best. Put on a household rubber glove, dampen it slightly, and drag your hand across the seat or carpet in one direction. The rubber grips the hair and rolls it into clumps you can pick up or hoover. Rinse the glove as it loads up.

### A rubber pet hair brush or squeegee

Same principle, a bit faster on big areas like the boot floor. Short, firm strokes towards you, then hoover the pile.

### A pumice-style pet hair stone

These work well on tough carpet but go carefully. They're abrasive, so test them on a hidden bit first and keep them off delicate seat fabric and anything leather.

### A good hoover with a narrow nozzle

After loosening, hoover slowly. A narrow crevice tool gets into the seams of the seats and the gap where the seat back meets the base, which is where a lot of hair ends up.

### Work in the right order

Seats first, then carpets, then the boot. Hair falls as you work, so do the high bits before the low bits. Do the seams and edges before the flat areas.

## What doesn't really work

- **Lint rollers.** Fine for a quick tidy of a seat before someone gets in. On a whole boot you'll be there all afternoon and get through a pack of refills.
- **Wiping with a damp cloth.** It just moves the hair around and mats it into the fabric.
- **Hoovering harder.** Going over the same spot with the hoover won't pull out hair that's woven in. Loosen first.
- **Fabric spray on top.** Making the carpet smell nicer doesn't take the hair out of it.

## Stopping it building up

Prevention is far less work than removal.

- **A washable boot liner** catches most of it. Take it out and shake it after a muddy trip.
- **A seat cover** for the back seat if the dog travels there.
- **Brush the dog** before a long trip, especially in moulting season.
- **A quick hoover every week or two** stops hair working its way down into the carpet in the first place.
- **Towel the dog off** after the beach. Wet hair sticks harder, and sand in the carpet is its own problem.
