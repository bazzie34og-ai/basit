If you've ever pulled out of a car wash, looked at the bonnet in the sun and seen a web of fine circles that weren't there before, you're not imagining it. Swirl marks and light scratches are one of the most common complaints people make about car washes, here in Aberdeen and everywhere else.

The good news is that they're not random. They have causes, and nearly all of those causes are avoidable. This note explains what's going on and how a proper hand wash gets round it. We won't name anyone or point fingers. Busy places cut corners, and the corners are always the same ones.

## What a scratch from washing actually is

Your car's paint has a clear coat on top. It's tough, but it's thinner than most people think, and it marks easily.

A swirl mark is a very fine scratch in that clear coat. On its own you'd never see it. Thousands of them, all running in circles where a cloth or sponge went round and round, catch the light and show up as that cobweb effect, especially on dark paint and especially in low winter sun.

The thing doing the scratching is almost never the sponge or the cloth itself. It's **grit**. Sand, road dust, brake dust and the fine gritty film that builds up on a car through a Scottish winter. Drag that across paint under pressure and it acts like very fine sandpaper.

So the whole question of whether a wash scratches comes down to one thing: **how much grit is on the paint when something rubs against it, and how much grit is on the thing doing the rubbing.**

## The usual culprits

### 1. No pre-wash

A car arrives covered in road film. If the first thing that touches it is a sponge, that sponge is pushing all of that grit around the panels. Every wipe is a light scrub with sand.

A pre-wash does the opposite. It loosens and lifts as much dirt as possible *before* any contact, so it can be rinsed off. Skipping it saves a few minutes per car, which is why it gets skipped.

### 2. One sponge for everything

Wheels are the dirtiest part of a car by a distance. They're covered in brake dust, which is made of tiny metal particles, plus whatever came off the road. A sponge that's been round the wheels and then goes onto the doors is carrying all of that onto your paintwork.

The same goes for the lower sills, which catch everything thrown up off the road. Washing top to bottom, and keeping wheel kit away from paint, matters.

### 3. A dirty sponge or mitt that never gets rinsed

Even on a single panel, a wash mitt picks up grit. If it goes back into a bucket of dirty water, or never gets rinsed at all, it picks that grit straight back up and carries it to the next panel. By the end of a busy day the water in that bucket is basically liquid sand.

### 4. Drying with a dirty chamois

This is the one people notice. A chamois, or "shammy", dropped on the ground and picked back up. Or the same one used on fifty cars. Drying is a contact step, so anything stuck in the drying cloth gets dragged across the whole car, after it's clean. That's how you get scratches on a car that came out looking spotless.

### 5. Rushing

Most of the above comes down to time. When there's a queue out onto the road, every minute matters to the business, and the steps that protect your paint are the ones that take minutes. It's not usually that anyone's careless. It's that the job is set up for speed.

## How a proper hand wash avoids it

There's nothing magic about it. It's the same steps in a sensible order, done without skipping any. Here's how our [Exterior Valet](/exterior-valet-aberdeen/) runs, straight off our own price list:

1. **Pre-wash.** Loosen the worst of the road film first.
2. **Wheel and tyre clean.** Get the dirtiest part done early, so that dirt isn't anywhere near clean paint later.
3. **Snow foam.** A thick foam that clings to the car, softens the dirt that's left, and lets it run off. If you've seen the foam on the paving slabs in our photos, that's this step.
4. **Thorough hand wash.** Only now does a mitt touch the paint, once most of the grit has already gone.
5. **Full rinse.** Everything off.
6. **Microfibre dry.** Microfibre is designed to trap particles in its fibres rather than drag them across the surface. That's the opposite of an old chamois.
7. **Wax spray finish.** Adds a layer that helps the next lot of muck slide off rather than stick.
8. **Tyres dressed.**

The order is the important bit. The contact steps, the hand wash and the dry, come *after* everything that removes grit. Nothing rubs against your paint until most of what could scratch it has been rinsed away.

## Can a hand wash still scratch?

Honestly, yes, if it's done badly. A hand wash with one dirty bucket and a sponge off the floor will scratch a car as well as anything. "Hand wash" on a sign doesn't mean much on its own. What matters is the process: pre-wash first, clean kit, contact last, microfibre to dry.

If you're paying anyone to wash your car, it's fair to ask what order they do things in. A good valeter won't mind the question.

## Can scratches that are already there be fixed?

Light swirl marks are in the top of the clear coat, and taking them out properly is machine polishing, which is a separate, specialist job. A wax finish can make them a little less obvious by filling the surface slightly, but it won't remove them. Deeper scratches that catch your fingernail are through more of the paint and are a body shop job.

The best thing you can do for paint you're happy with is to stop new swirls going in. That means washing it the careful way from now on.

## What you can do at home

If you wash your own car, a few changes make a big difference:

- **Rinse first, thoroughly**, before you touch anything.
- **Do the wheels first** and use separate kit for them.
- **Rinse your mitt often** and change the water when it's grey.
- **Never use anything that's touched the ground.** If the mitt hits the driveway, it's done for the day.
- **Dry with clean microfibre**, not a chamois that's lived in the boot since 2019.
- **Work in straight lines**, not circles, so any marks you do make are less visible.
