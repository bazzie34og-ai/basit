You’ll see both terms everywhere: “steam cleaning” and “hot water extraction”. Some companies use them as if they mean the same thing. Others talk about them as if they’re rivals. Our own posts say “deep steam and hot water extraction”, so it’s only fair we explain what we mean.

Here’s the honest version.

## The short answer

When people talk about **steam cleaning a carpet**, they almost always mean **hot water extraction**. It’s the everyday name for it. Hot water extraction is the more accurate, technical name.

Hot water extraction means we spray hot water and cleaning solution deep into the fibres, then suck it straight back out with the dirt.

There’s also something called **dry steam cleaning**, which is a different thing altogether. It’s used on hard surfaces and for some spot work, but rarely to clean whole carpets.

## Where the name “steam cleaning” comes from

Carpet cleaning machines heat water to a high temperature. When that hot water hits the carpet, especially on a cold day, you can see vapour rising. It looks like steam, and for years people have called it steam cleaning.

The water isn’t usually turned fully into steam. It’s hot water, sprayed under pressure and immediately vacuumed back up. But the name stuck, and it’s what most people search for. If you ask for “carpet steam cleaning in Cardiff”, hot water extraction is what you’ll get from most professional cleaners, including us.

## How hot water extraction works

A professional clean usually goes like this.

1. **Dry soil removal.** A thorough hoover first. Most dirt in a carpet is dry grit, and it’s easier to remove dry than wet.
2. **Pre-treatment.** A cleaning solution matched to the carpet and the stains, sprayed on and left to work for a few minutes. This loosens oily dirt and stains so they release easily.
3. **Extraction.** The wand sprays hot water into the pile and, in the same stroke, sucks it straight back out along with the loosened dirt. You can see the dirty water in the machine.
4. **Dry passes.** Extra passes with the wand, without spraying, to pull out more moisture.
5. **Drying.** Air movers if needed, and airflow in the room.

The stripes you see on a carpet afterwards are the passes of the wand. They brush out as the pile dries.

## What dry steam is

Dry steam machines heat water to well above boiling point and release a low-moisture vapour. The steam is hot enough to loosen grease and grime on hard surfaces, and there’s very little water left behind.

It’s good for:

- tiles, grout and hard floors
- kitchen surfaces, hobs and extractor hoods
- bathroom fittings and shower screens
- some spot work on upholstery and mattresses

It isn’t usually used to clean a whole carpet, because steam on its own loosens dirt but doesn’t remove it. Without strong suction to carry the dirt away, it just moves around.

## Side by side

**Hot water extraction**

- Removes dirt from deep in the pile, rather than moving it about
- Rinses out stains, residue and old cleaning products
- Suits most carpets, rugs and fabric upholstery
- Leaves the carpet damp, not wet, usually dry in a few hours

**Dry steam**

- Very little moisture
- Great for hard surfaces, grout and grease
- Useful for some spot treatments
- Not designed to rinse and remove dirt from a whole carpet

## What about germ-killing claims?

You’ll often see cleaning companies claim their method kills almost all germs. Heat and some solutions do reduce bacteria, but the real figure depends on the temperature at the carpet surface, how long it’s held there and the product used. We don’t make that claim unless we can back it up with the product’s own test data.

What we can say is that hot water extraction physically removes a great deal of dirt, dust, skin, hair and spills from deep in the carpet, which is what makes it look and smell fresher.

## Hire machines versus professional machines

Supermarket hire machines use the same idea, spraying and sucking back. The big differences are heat and suction. A professional machine is much stronger, so it gets more dirt out and leaves less water behind. Less water means faster drying and less chance of marks wicking back up.

The most common problem we see after a DIY clean is a carpet that went grey again quickly. That’s usually soap left in the pile. It’s sticky, and it attracts dirt. A proper rinse removes it.

## Can extraction damage a carpet?

Done badly, any wet method can cause problems. The usual culprits are too much water, water that’s too hot for the fibre, or a strong product that isn’t rinsed out. These can lead to shrinkage on some natural-fibre carpets, colour run on some rugs, brown marks wicking up from the backing, and carpets that take days to dry.

That’s why the method matters less than how it’s done. A careful cleaner checks the carpet type first, tests a hidden spot, adjusts the temperature and the solution, and makes extra dry passes to pull out as much water as possible. If something isn’t safe to clean with water, they tell you, rather than finding out the hard way.

## Which one do you need?

For carpets, rugs, sofas and most mattresses, the answer is hot water extraction, whatever you call it. For kitchens, bathrooms and hard surfaces in a deep clean, steam has a useful place.
